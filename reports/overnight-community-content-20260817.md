## Original post bank

### Problem-led

**1 — The “interesting grant” trap**  
Many small nonprofits lose time on grants that look relevant but fail one basic test: the work, geography, applicant type, or timing does not match. Our first-pass screen is five questions: Who can apply? Where must the work happen? What outcome is funded? What is the total effort? What is the deadline and decision date? If one answer is unclear, we park the opportunity until the source clarifies it. What is your fastest disqualifier?

**2 — A deadline is not a plan**  
Working backward from the due date is useful, but the real planning date is earlier: the date you can still get partner sign-off, budget numbers, board approval, and attachments without rushing. We now label those internal dates separately from the funder deadline. It makes “on time” measurable before the application becomes urgent.

### Educational

**3 — FOA to plain-language brief**  
Try this translation exercise before assigning a writer: (1) copy the official purpose, (2) write the eligible applicant in one sentence, (3) list required activities, (4) list required evidence, (5) name what is explicitly out of scope, and (6) record the official questions/contact path. Keep the official link beside every interpretation. This reduces confident guesses disguised as requirements.

**4 — Direct, indirect, and uncovered cost**  
Before building a grant budget, separate costs into three buckets: costs clearly tied to the project, shared costs allocated by a documented method, and real costs the award will not cover. The third bucket matters: an award can be mission-aligned and still financially incomplete. Use the funder’s rules and a qualified finance professional for the final treatment.

### Checklist

**5 — Five-minute opportunity triage**  
Save an opportunity only when you can answer: applicant eligibility; geography; program fit; award range; match/cost-share; deadline and time zone; reporting burden; and official application URL. Mark unknowns as unknown. A blank is a prompt for research, not permission to fill in a plausible answer.

**6 — Pre-submission attachment check**  
Before upload, verify: file type and page limits; organization name consistency; budget totals; dates; signatures; required registrations; accessible formatting; and that every link opens without a private login. Have someone unfamiliar with the draft read the instructions and locate each response. Fresh eyes catch missing “small” requirements.

### Partner-oriented

**7 — A better partner conversation**  
Instead of starting with “Can you be our partner?”, start with four concrete questions: What community need are we each seeing? Which part can each organization deliver? What evidence can each contribute? What would make the collaboration too burdensome? A clear no is useful information, and a clear yes has a better chance of becoming a workable plan.

**8 — Shared grant calendar, separate ownership**  
A coalition calendar works best when every entry has one owner, one source URL, one next decision, and one backup date. Shared visibility does not require shared passwords or one person carrying every follow-up. The simplest useful calendar is the one partners will keep current.

### Founder-learning

**9 — What we learned from “more features”**  
Our early instinct was to add more discovery features. The more useful lesson was to make the next decision smaller: fit, effort, source, and next action. A tool earns its place when it helps a busy team decide what not to pursue. We are still learning where automation should stop and human judgment should begin.

**10 — Trust is a product requirement**  
For grant work, a polished answer is less valuable than a traceable one. We prefer an explicit “unknown” with an official source link over a confident guess. That principle affects the interface, the content, and how we talk about results. What is one small trust rule your team has adopted?
